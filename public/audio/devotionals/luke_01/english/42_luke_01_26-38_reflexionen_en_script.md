## Reflection
On the surface, this is a story of divine intervention, a miraculous conception, and Mary's obedient acceptance. We see an angel, a promise, and a seemingly impossible situation. But look closer. Gabriel's greeting, 'Greetings, favored one!' – in the original Greek, *kecharitomene* – speaks of a grace already given, a favor already bestowed. Mary is addressed as someone already deeply loved and valued by God. Bovon's commentary highlights that, while the annunciation mirrors Zechariah's, Mary, a young woman of little social standing from an insignificant village – Fitzmyer notes Nazareth's small size – receives an even greater promise. Her question, 'How can this happen?' is not doubt, but a genuine inquiry, as Bock points out, seeking understanding. Then comes the pivotal moment. The Holy Spirit will *episkiasei* – overshadow – her. This word, echoing the cloud that covered the tabernacle in Exodus, suggests that Mary is to become the new locus of divine presence. But the real surprise is in Mary's response: 'Let it be to me according to your word.' This isn't blind obedience to a command from on high. It's a response rooted in something far deeper. Mary doesn't say, 'I will *do* this for the Lord.' She says, in essence, 'I *belong* to the Lord; let it be.' Her 'yes' flows from her identity, from a settled sense of belonging to God, not from a sense of duty or obligation. This inverts our usual logic. We often think we must *do* the right things, prove our faith, *earn* our belonging. Mary shows us a different path: know *whose* you are, and then act from that place. The impossible becomes possible not through striving, but through settled belonging. Mary's courageous faith – knowing that her 'yes' could lead to social catastrophe, as she was betrothed and pregnancy outside of marriage would have been severely punished – stemmed from a deep conviction that she already belonged to God, that she was already held in God's grace.
