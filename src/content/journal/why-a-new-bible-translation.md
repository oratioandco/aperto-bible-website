There are already many Bible translations. Ancient and modern. Formal and dynamic. Beloved and controversial. Designed for study, devotion, public reading, liturgy, or outreach.

So why create another one?

That question has followed Aperto from the beginning.

The first answer was practical: we needed a high-quality, license-free Bible translation for a Bible app project. But as the project developed, the question became larger.

What would a Bible translation look like if it were designed from the beginning for modern readers, digital reading habits, audio engagement, multilingual access, and honest questions?

What if it could be easy to read without becoming thin?

What if it could be beautiful without becoming obscure?

What if it could preserve the Bible's literary force, historical distance, and sometimes uncomfortable strangeness — while still opening the door for newcomers, skeptics, and long-time believers alike?

That is the vision behind Aperto.

Aperto comes from Latin and means **open**.

Open Scripture. Open context. Open questions. Open access. Open across languages.

## The translation dilemma

Every Bible translation lives with a tension.

More literal translations often preserve the structure and texture of the original languages. They can be incredibly valuable. But without cultural, historical, literary, and linguistic context, they can also be misread. A phrase may look clear in modern language while carrying ancient assumptions, idioms, or social meanings that are no longer obvious. In that sense, literalness does not automatically equal clarity.

Without that context, a literal translation can feel accurate but distant.

On the other side, more dynamic or thought-for-thought translations aim to make the Bible easier to understand. They smooth difficult sentences, clarify ideas, and help modern readers follow the flow of the text.

> Literalness does not automatically equal clarity.

But sometimes, in making the text easier to read, something else gets lost: the ruggedness, tension, irony, beauty, awkwardness, repetition, poetry, or sharpness of the original.

The Bible is not one kind of book. It is law, poetry, prophecy, gospel, apocalypse, wisdom, lament, history, letter, prayer, song, and argument. Sometimes it is polished. Sometimes it is raw. Sometimes it is deliberately strange.

Aperto is trying to hold that tension differently.

## Literary equivalence

The vision for Aperto is a translation in modern language that is easy to read, but not simplistic.

We want to render the beauty, force, and literary texture of the biblical writings for modern readers. I think of this as **literary equivalence**.

That means we are not only asking, "What do these words mean?"

We are also asking:

- What kind of writing is this?
- What effect did it have on its original recipients?
- What tone does it carry?
- Is it elevated, simple, poetic, abrupt, ironic, humorous, political, intimate, or confrontational?
- What would a comparable literary experience feel like for readers today?

For example, Luke opens his Gospel with polished, high-register Greek that signals careful historical writing. Then he shifts into a more familiar narrative style. That movement matters. It tells us something about Luke's purpose, audience, and craft.

A translation should not erase that.

Aperto aims to be readable in modern language while still preserving the contours of the original texts — including their beauty, their weight, and sometimes their rough edges.

## Illuminations

One of the core ideas behind Aperto is what we call **Illuminations**.

Illuminations are short, clearly marked elements woven into the reading experience that help modern readers notice what original audiences may have understood immediately.

The guiding question is:

> What would the original recipients have readily understood that many modern readers miss?

That question matters because modern readers often come to the Bible with very different starting points. Many do not have inherited biblical literacy. They may not know the larger storyline, the religious vocabulary, the geography, the genres, the temple practices, the political tensions, or the echoes of earlier Scripture that biblical writers often assume.

And even long-time readers can miss what would have been obvious in the original world of the text.

Illuminations are designed to bridge that gap without turning the Bible into a textbook. They can highlight things like:

- cultural assumptions
- religious practices
- political tensions
- social status
- irony and humor
- echoes of earlier Scripture
- historical background
- honor and shame dynamics
- geography
- wordplay
- literary structure

The goal is not to interrupt the Bible with constant explanation. The goal is to illuminate what is already there.

Some things belong directly in the translation. Some things need a short contextual cue. Some things are better explored in footnotes, articles, audio guides, or study resources.

But the principle is the same: **modern readers should not have to become ancient historians before they can begin to understand what is happening in the text.**

Aperto wants to open the door.

## Honest notes for difficult passages

![A hand-thrown ceramic bowl, visibly broken and carefully repaired with metal staples, standing whole in warm window light](/images/journal/section-honest-notes.jpeg)

Aperto also includes a richer approach to notes.

Many Bible readers have questions that traditional study notes do not always address openly. Some passages have been used to harm people. Some are morally difficult. Some raise historical or scientific questions. Some are read very differently across Christian traditions. Some are deeply meaningful to believers and deeply troubling to skeptics.

We do not want to hide those tensions.

One category of Aperto notes is what we call **Harm History**: places where we openly acknowledge how certain passages have been used to justify violence, exclusion, antisemitism, racism, misogyny, colonialism, abuse, or other forms of harm.

That does not mean the text is reduced to its misuse. But it does mean that responsible reading includes honesty about reception history.

Other notes will map the breadth of interpretation across Christian traditions. Where there are significant differences, we want to show how believers, scholars, and skeptics have engaged with these passages constructively.

Aperto is not designed to win arguments by hiding complexity. It is designed to invite readers into Scripture with intellectual honesty, humility, and trust.

## Designed for how people actually read today

Aperto also begins with a sobering observation: many people do not read the Bible the way churches often imagine they do.

I grew up in evangelical contexts where there was a deep love for Scripture. That love was real. But it was often paired with strong expectations around daily Bible reading, long quiet times, Bible-in-a-year plans, and benchmarks that could easily become a kind of performative spirituality.

Digital reading patterns tell a different story.

Digital access has made Bible engagement more frequent and more available. A person can open Scripture on a phone in a train, during a break, before bed, in a moment of anxiety, or after a conversation. That matters.

But digital reading also tends to be shorter, more fragmented, and more easily interrupted. People may engage more often, but not necessarily more deeply. Screen-based reading can encourage scanning over sustained attention, and readers may retain less when they move quickly through disconnected passages.

Many people also engage the Bible affectively. They search for passages that connect with their emotions, circumstances, anxieties, hopes, grief, questions, or season of life.

Public data from platforms such as YouVersion and Bible Gateway reflects this. Their annual reports and popular-verse lists have often shown strong engagement with passages about fear, anxiety, hope, comfort, guidance, and trust. People do not usually begin with abstract questions about canon, genre, or historical context. They often begin with something they are carrying.

This does not have to be dismissed as shallow. It may be the doorway through which someone begins to read. But it does mean that a Bible reading experience should help people move from a resonant verse into the larger passage, story, and world of the text.

At the same time, digital readers increasingly expect interaction. They expect to be able to tap a word, expand a note, listen to a passage, follow a theme, compare translations, search naturally, or ask a question. As AI becomes part of ordinary reading and learning, readers will also expect more conversational ways of exploring Scripture.

Aperto is designed for that reality — but not by turning the Bible into a feed of isolated inspirational fragments, and not by replacing careful reading with instant answers.

Instead, Aperto is designed to help readers move:

- from entry point to context
- from verse to pericope
- from felt need to deeper reading
- from quick access to sustained attention
- from passive consumption to active exploration
- from isolated questions to grounded, trustworthy guidance

That means we care about:

- short reading sessions
- meaningful literary units
- search behavior
- emotional and thematic entry points
- expandable notes
- word-level explanations
- audio engagement
- guided pathways
- contextual resources that do not overwhelm
- beautiful language that can be read aloud
- AI-assisted exploration grounded in rigorous scholarship
- resources for both skeptics and believers

The goal is not to lower the value of deep study. It is to create more doorways into it.

## Reading in meaningful units

![A round loaf of bread on linen, torn into natural pieces along its own seams rather than sliced](/images/journal/section-meaningful-units.jpeg)

One practical way Aperto responds to digital fragmentation is by rethinking how the biblical text is presented and navigated.

> Aperto presents Scripture as meaningful literary units, not an endless stream of numbered sentences.

Book, chapter, and verse references are useful. Aperto will continue to support them because they help readers find, cite, compare, and study passages. But chapters and verses are not the Bible's original literary structure. They were added later as reference systems.

When they become the primary way of reading, Scripture can feel fragmented — like an endless stream of numbered sentences rather than stories, poems, letters, arguments, visions, prayers, and carefully shaped literary units.

Aperto therefore gives greater attention to the natural units of the text: scenes, speeches, poems, paragraphs, arguments, narrative movements, and pericopes.

In digital and print, Aperto is designed to help readers encounter one meaningful unit at a time. A Gospel story can be read as a scene. A psalm can be encountered as a poem. A prophetic oracle can stand with its own force. A section of Paul's argument can be followed as an argument, not chopped into isolated proof-texts.

The goal is not to remove references, but to restore context.

## Designed for the ear, not only the eye

![A small weathered bronze bell hanging on a cord in a bright open window, its shadow cast on the stucco wall](/images/journal/section-for-the-ear.jpeg)

The Bible was heard before it was silently read.

Before personal Bibles, reading plans, apps, and verse images, Scripture was encountered through public reading, teaching, song, prayer, and memory.

Today, audio is becoming central again. People engage Scripture through audio Bibles, devotionals, podcasts, guided meditations, music, and liturgy. For many, listening is not secondary. It is their main mode of engagement.

That means a modern Bible translation needs to be designed not only for the page, but also for the ear.

Aperto pays attention to:

- cadence
- sentence length
- density
- rhythm
- oral clarity
- emotional tone
- performance and narration
- how a passage sounds when read aloud

A sentence can be technically accurate and still nearly impossible to listen to. A paragraph can be readable on screen but exhausting in audio.

If Scripture is to be encountered in digital and audio-first environments, the translation itself must be shaped with that reality in mind.

## Multilingual from the start

![One clay pitcher on a wooden table surrounded by several handmade cups, each visibly different in shape and glaze](/images/journal/section-multilingual.jpeg)

Many excellent Bible translation projects, digital Bible tools, and faith platforms come from the United States. Many of them are high quality, deeply resourced, and have served millions of people.

But they are often English-first. Sometimes they are English-only. And many are built around cultural assumptions that do not fully translate into other contexts.

Aperto begins from a European perspective, but not in order to become Eurocentric.

Europe itself is not one context. Its countries and regions carry very different histories of church, secularization, public religion, biblical memory, and skepticism. But working from Europe makes certain questions difficult to avoid. What does Bible engagement look like when inherited biblical literacy can no longer be assumed? What happens when readers do not know the basic storyline, the genres, the vocabulary, or the cultural world of Scripture? What if the first challenge is not choosing the right translation, but helping someone feel that the Bible can be opened at all?

These questions are not only European. They increasingly matter in the English-speaking world as well. But they are especially visible in many European contexts, and they have shaped Aperto from the beginning.

The vision is to leverage AI, rigorous scholarly processes, and deep theological resources to create a translation and ecosystem that can serve many languages without making English the center of everything.

We are beginning with German, Polish, and English.

The goal is not to translate from English outward, as if English were the source. The goal is to build a process where local languages, local readers, and local partners matter from the beginning.

Aperto is open by design: open in access, open in posture, open to questions, and open to collaboration.

## An open invitation

Aperto is not being created because every other Bible translation has failed.

It is being created because the way people encounter Scripture is changing — and because many people have never had a truly open invitation to begin.

We need translations that are accurate, beautiful, accessible, and honest.

We need reading experiences that take ancient context seriously without making readers feel excluded.

We need tools that fit digital and audio habits without reducing Scripture to fragments.

We need multilingual resources that are not simply exported from an English-speaking center.

And we need space to acknowledge that the Bible has been a source of profound hope, beauty, justice, and transformation — and also that it has sometimes been misused in ways that caused real harm.

Aperto is one attempt to hold all of that together.

A modern-language, literary-equivalent, context-rich, digitally native, openly accessible Bible translation.

It is an invitation.

For the person who has never opened the Bible.

For the person who left because the Bible was used against them.

For the skeptic who still has honest questions.

For the believer who wants to read familiar words with fresh attention.

For churches, creators, educators, and local partners who need open, beautiful, trustworthy Scripture resources in their own language.

Aperto means open.

And that is what we hope this project becomes:

an open door into Scripture.
