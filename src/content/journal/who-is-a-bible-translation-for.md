Picture a man on a Berlin commuter train, earbuds in, scrolling past a notification that says he's three days behind on his reading plan. In that moment, scripture has been encountered once — fragmented, private, anxious — through the single mode the app offers. A Benedictine monk in the same morning hours would have encountered it eight times: sung, spoken, heard, held in silence. Formation was never just reading. But the tools that should serve this man's formation treat him as a user to retain.

That sentence is the conclusion of my master's research on digital spiritual formation, and it's the reason Aperto exists. I spent a year mapping how Bible and prayer apps actually work, and the pattern is consistent: every wave of faith-tech has imported the reigning tech paradigm of its day — and since the smartphone era, that means attention-economy mechanics. Streaks, notifications, completion rewards — inherited wholesale, without anyone systematically asking whether they serve formation or merely drive retention.

**Formed or managed**

The distinction that organizes everything I found is this: a user who opens a devotional each morning because yesterday's spoke into her life has been *formed*. A user who opens it because breaking a 47-day streak feels like failure has been *managed*. The frequency is identical. The mechanism is entirely different — and the research is not kind to the second one. Streak-based systems measurably increase anxiety, guilt, and dependency; broken streaks create guilt, not grace. Performance spirituality digitized.

This critique is starting to break into the mainstream. Freya India's recent essay ["The Commodification of Christianity"](https://www.freyaindia.co.uk/p/the-commodification-of-christianity) makes it beautifully — XP for scripture, "what if learning the faith felt addictive?" as an actual pitch — and lands on the observation I care most about: for an established believer, these apps are a supplement. For a seeker, they are the *primary encounter*. Whatever the app optimizes for is what the seeker's faith becomes.

**The people the apps assume don't exist**

Here's who I mean by seeker, from the research. She grew up Catholic, left during university, and hasn't set foot in a church in twelve years — but she still prays sometimes, usually in crisis. He deconstructed his evangelical faith after a church split and reads theology blogs late at night, looking for something that holds together. They are not atheists. They are not believers in any settled sense. They are in between — and there are far more of them than the apps assume.

In Germany, where I build, the numbers are stark: among young adults *inside* the Protestant church, 58% describe themselves as "not religious." Forty percent of people with no church affiliation at all say they find biblical content interesting — and 56% have never opened a Bible. Across the secular West, that gap between interest and engagement runs to a couple hundred million people. It is not a demand problem. It is a design problem: existing apps assume a user who already knows what they want, and serve libraries to people who need a guide.

**What building for them actually requires**

Aperto — from the Latin for "open" — is my answer, and since this is the first post on this journal, let me say plainly what it is. It's a new Bible translation, made from the Hebrew and Greek into contemporary German, English, and Polish: drafted by AI, checked by a panel of adversarial AI reviewers, and decided verse by verse by a human — with every decision documented in public. Around the text grows an apparatus for people who weren't raised on this book: footnotes that explain the culture and admit disputed readings; audio versions; songs and podcasts grown from the same research. And on that foundation we're building a personal companion — a short daily audio practice of scripture, reflection, and prayer that knows where you are on your way, currently in testing with first users.

All of it is built for exactly that threshold reader, and the commitments are checkable, not vibes:

**The text is free and stays free.** Published under a Creative Commons license, commercial use included. No paywall on scripture, no lifetime pricing on the gospel.

**Grace-based mechanics, all the way down.** No streaks, no shame triggers, no manufactured urgency. The test I apply to every feature: does this serve freedom or fear? Grace or guilt? Formation or metrics? And the hardest question in my framework, the one I make myself answer: *if someone developed a deep practice and no longer needed what we build, would we celebrate or mourn?* When I first applied that question to Aperto, the honest answer was uncomfortable: part of me mourned. That discomfort is exactly why the question has to stay.

**The friction stays in.** Commodified faith content is smooth — comfort verses, soft focus. Our translation's north star cuts the other way: the modern reader should be hit by the text the way the original audience was, same clarity *and* same friction. Mary's song in Luke 1 — a poem about rulers pulled from thrones and the rich sent away empty — stays political, because it is. And the apparatus tells the whole truth — our footnotes include a category for where these very texts have been misused. The commercial risk of challenging content is real. But the ethical risk of comfortable content is documented.

**Personalization that forms, not flatters.** An AI that learns what a user likes and serves more of it is a recommendation engine, not a formation companion. The difference between formation-driven and preference-driven personalization is the difference between a spiritual director and a Netflix algorithm.

**Is that enough?**

No — and the research says so too. A well-crafted paragraph about confession, however warm and theologically correct, is not a hand on your shoulder from someone who will see you again next week. No algorithm produces presence. What a translation and the tools around it can be is a bridge: designed for community connection without claiming to be community, scaffolding toward the table without making the table a prerequisite. If our metrics ever show people substituting this for embodied community rather than crossing toward it, that's not a business success to celebrate — that's the failure condition, named in advance.

These commitments come out of a design framework I'm still working out — this journal is where I'll test its principles against reality, one post at a time. The methodology behind the translation itself is public today: [the method](/en/methodology), [the text](/en/), and here, where the wins and the failures both go on the record.
