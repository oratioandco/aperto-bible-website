# Luke -- Introduction

## What Kind of Book Is This?

Most people who pick up a Gospel for the first time bring the wrong expectations. They expect either a factual report -- eyewitness testimony, journalistic accuracy, verifiable claims -- or religious propaganda dressed up as history. Luke is neither.

What Luke wrote belongs to an ancient genre called *bios* -- a life. Plutarch wrote them. Suetonius, Tacitus. The conventions of this genre are unfamiliar to us now, but they were clear to the original audience: you select episodes that distill the essence of a life. You arrange material not by calendar but by argument. You compose speeches -- not as fabrication, but as recognized literary practice. What would this person have said in this situation? Every ancient historian worked this way, and their readers knew it.

Luke says so himself in the opening four sentences. He has investigated, consulted sources, interviewed eyewitnesses, and is now writing an "orderly account" for someone named Theophilus. Orderly does not mean chronological. It means: told with a purpose.

## Who Wrote It

The author never names himself. "Luke" comes from a second-century tradition -- possibly the physician mentioned briefly in Paul's letters. The attribution may be right. It may not. Scholars disagree. What the text itself reveals: its author was educated, wrote polished Greek, knew the Jewish scriptures in their Greek translation, and was probably not Jewish. He wrote for readers who had never set foot in Judea and needed everything explained -- customs, power dynamics, religious logic.

The likely date of composition falls between 80 and 95 CE, roughly fifty years after the events described. And there is a sequel: the book of Acts is the second volume of the same project. Luke tells a story that does not end at Easter.

## How Ancient Biography Works

Three things the original audience knew that you need to know too.

First: speeches in ancient texts are not transcripts. No ancient author claimed to reproduce word-for-word what someone said. He rendered what this person would likely have said in this situation -- distilled, sharpened, brought to a point. Thucydides did it. Luke did it. It was not deception. It was a different contract between writer and reader.

Second: numbers often carry symbolic weight. Twelve apostles, seventy messengers, forty days in the wilderness -- these are not coincidences but meaning-carriers that a Jewish-educated audience would have recognized immediately.

Third: selection is interpretation. Luke does not tell everything that happened. He selects, condenses, omits -- and it is precisely in this selection that his argument lives.

## The Key Idea

Every book has an argument. Luke's can be stated in a single sentence: the outsider is always the insider.

Shepherds -- the lowest-status workers in first-century Judea -- are the first to hear about the birth. A Samaritan, from the ethnic group Jews most despised, becomes the definition of compassion. A tax collector -- collaborator with the occupying empire, hated by his own people -- is welcomed before the religious elite even gets a word in. Women, who could not serve as witnesses in any court, are the first witnesses to the resurrection.

This is not accidental. It is systematic. Already in the first chapter, one character declares: "He has pulled the powerful down from their thrones and lifted up the lowly." That single line is the key to the entire book. Keep it in mind, and you will find that nearly every story resolves into it.

## How It Moves

The story follows a geography. It begins in the Temple in Jerusalem -- the religious and political center -- moves outward into the rural Galilean north, then returns in a long, slow arc to Jerusalem. That arc, stretching across roughly chapters 9 through 19, is the heart of the book: Jesus walking toward a city where he knows what awaits him. Along the way, he teaches, tells parables, heals, argues. Luke is in no rush. The journey is not the prelude to the real action -- the journey *is* the action.

The opening chapters tell two birth stories side by side: a prophet and the figure who will change everything. Then comes Galilee -- healings, conflicts with authorities, the gathering of an unlikely community. The long road to Jerusalem deepens the question of who belongs in God's project. And at the end: not triumph, but a meal, an empty tomb, and instructions to wait. An ending that opens.

## Why Read It Now

In the English-speaking world, the Bible has a specific problem: familiarity. Not the kind that comes from actually reading it -- the kind that comes from cultural osmosis. The Christmas story in a holiday pageant. A few parables from school. The general arc absorbed through movies, novels, political rhetoric. Enough to feel confident you know what it says. Enough to feel confident it has nothing to say to you.

Anyone who actually reads Luke will lose that confidence quickly. The book is more political than expected. More radical. Stranger. The reversals it describes are not the harmless stories of a children's Bible -- they are systematic challenges to who deserves power, who belongs, and what wealth does to a person. These are not first-century questions. They are as unanswered in Brooklyn, Chicago, or San Francisco as they were in Galilee.

Luke wrote for people who had no reason to believe any of it. His audience were educated Greeks and Romans asking a practical question: Who was this person, and why does he still matter fifty years later? If you are reading this text without a church background -- without a denomination, without a childhood confirmation class, without any of the institutional baggage -- you are exactly the audience it was written for.

## About This Translation

The King James Version shaped the English language itself. But its phrases -- *and it came to pass*, *verily I say unto you*, *behold* -- signal one thing to modern readers: this is not for you. Not because the text is irrelevant, but because the language locks it in a past where it does not belong.

On the other end, contemporary evangelical translations carry their own kind of weight. They read as "a Bible" -- recognizable, categorized, aimed at church audiences even when they claim otherwise. The vocabulary is subtly coded: a reader who has left church culture, or never entered it, can hear it within a paragraph.

The Aperto Bible translates Luke into contemporary literary English -- prose that aims to be taken seriously as literature, and for exactly that reason opens a different way into the text. No archaisms performing solemnity. No committee-produced blandness. No worship-culture vocabulary. Instead, what the best translations have always done: brought the text into the language people actually think, argue, and tell stories in. Now. Today.
